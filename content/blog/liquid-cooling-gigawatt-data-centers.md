**AI server racks now draw so much power, often tens of kilowatts and in next-generation designs reportedly hundreds, that air can no longer carry the heat away, so liquid cooling is becoming the default for new AI data centres.** Most new builds use direct-to-chip cooling, with cold plates sitting on the processors, while some use full immersion. And the biggest AI campuses are now planned in gigawatts of power, not megawatts.

## The three main approaches

- **Direct-to-chip:** liquid flows through cold plates mounted on CPUs and GPUs. It's the most common approach in new AI deployments.
- **Immersion:** whole servers sit in a non-conductive fluid. It's very efficient, but needs different hardware and operations.
- **Rear-door heat exchangers:** liquid-cooled doors on racks. A bridge for older data centres.

## How fast is adoption?

Estimates vary a lot between sources, from about a third to over 40% of new AI data-centre capacity using liquid cooling in 2026, with some hyperscaler projects mandating it outright. The direction is clear even if the exact number isn't: air cooling can't keep up with modern AI hardware.

## Why gigawatts?

A large AI campus can now need as much power as a city. That's why data-centre siting increasingly depends on power and water availability, and why long equipment lead times (for transformers especially) have become a bottleneck.

## Why it matters to everyone building with AI

1. **Cooling and power, not just chips, limit AI capacity.** That affects how quickly AI prices fall.
2. **Efficiency matters.** Better cooling means more compute per unit of energy, which shows up eventually as cheaper inference.
3. **Location matters.** Countries with power, water and cooler climates, and increasingly India with its data-centre boom, attract AI infrastructure.
